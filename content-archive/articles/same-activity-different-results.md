I gave the exact same project to five different fifth-grade classes.

The results surprised me.

The project was simple. I asked students to design an A4 poster inviting my foreign friends to visit Taiwan. They needed to choose four things they wanted to introduce about Taiwan and explain why people should visit.

Students could choose anything they thought represented Taiwan. Some focused on food, while others chose places, festivals, animals, or other things they personally liked.

At that time, we were learning about landmarks around the world, so I wanted to connect the lesson to something closer to their own lives. I also wanted them to think about what they would share if someone from another country asked them about Taiwan.

## The Class I Did Not Expect

One of my quieter classes was usually easier to teach. Students followed instructions, the classroom was organized, and lessons usually went smoothly.

I expected this activity to work well with them.

However, their posters surprised me.

Many students chose very similar topics. A lot of them introduced bubble tea, stinky tofu, or Taipei 101. Some posters looked almost the same as my example, and a few students told me they could not think of anything else.

Looking back, I wondered if my examples had influenced their choices more than I expected. I provided examples because I wanted students to understand the task and have a place to start, especially since we only had one class period to explain the project and begin working.

Maybe with more time to discuss ideas before starting, some students would have explored more possibilities.

## The Class That Surprised Me

Then I tried the same project with another class.

This class was very different. They were louder, more energetic, and sometimes more difficult to manage. They had many opinions, and keeping the class focused was not always easy.

Honestly, I did not expect them to create my favorite posters.

But they did.

Their ideas showed much more of their personalities. One student introduced Taiwanese endemic animals. I had briefly mentioned them before, but I was happy to see that he connected Taiwan with something he personally cared about.

Another student introduced Taiwan's hot air balloon festival. A student who loved baseball chose to introduce Taiwanese baseball.

Some ideas were different from what I originally expected, but I enjoyed seeing them bring their own interests into the project.

What made me even happier was their attitude. They were excited about their posters and wanted me to share them with my foreign friends.

Even students who were usually more playful became focused during the activity.

## The Same Project, Different Experiences

After this project, I started paying more attention to how different each class could be.

The instructions were the same. The activity was the same. The teacher was the same.

But the classroom atmosphere changed everything.

I used to hope that once I designed a good activity, it could work the same way in every class. It would definitely make teaching easier.

Over time, I realized that was unrealistic.

Each class has its own personality. Sometimes students influence each other. When many students are interested, that energy spreads throughout the classroom.

I would not call the quieter class a failure. They completed the task, and their posters still showed things they thought represented Taiwan.

The result was simply different from what I imagined.

For me, that was one of the interesting parts of teaching. Students do not always respond in the way I expect, and sometimes those differences are what make a classroom memorable.
