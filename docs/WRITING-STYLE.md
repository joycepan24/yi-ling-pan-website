# Writing Style Guide

> Version 1.1  
> Purpose: Long-term writing guide for a personal education website

This guide defines the author's writing voice and provides rules for editing raw material into polished English articles.

The goal is **not** to make every article sound polished, academic, inspirational, or professionally branded.

The goal is to make the writing sound like the author, with clearer English and stronger structure.

When a style rule conflicts with clarity, natural flow, or the author's original meaning, prioritize:

1. The author's meaning and factual accuracy
2. Natural, readable English
3. The author's voice
4. Article structure and flow
5. Individual style rules

Do not follow this guide mechanically.

---

# 1. Core Voice

The author's natural voice is:

- direct
- personal
- practical
- reflective
- curious
- grounded in real classroom experience
- comfortable admitting uncertainty
- occasionally humorous or mildly sarcastic
- more interested in what actually happened than in presenting an ideal version of teaching

The author does not need to sound like an education expert in every article.

She often writes from the position of:

> This is what I tried.  
> This is what happened.  
> This is what I noticed.  
> This is what I think about it now.

She may have an opinion without having a final answer.

Preserve that uncertainty when it is genuine.

---

# 2. The Most Important Editing Principle

Improve the English freely.

Improve the structure when necessary.

Do **not** improve the author's experience into something more impressive than it actually was.

A polished article should feel like:

> "This is what I would write if my English writing were stronger."

It should not feel like:

> "This is what a professional education content writer would write for me."

---

# 3. Raw Material Comes First

Raw material may be:

- English
- Chinese
- mixed Chinese and English
- grammatically incomplete
- repetitive
- informal
- written as bullet points
- emotionally direct
- poorly organized

This is acceptable.

Do not judge the author's intended voice from grammar mistakes or limited English vocabulary.

When the author provides Chinese or mixed-language raw material, first identify the intended meaning, attitude, level of certainty, and emotional tone.

Then express that meaning naturally in English.

Do not translate Chinese sentence structure literally.

Do not use the translation process as permission to make the thought more sophisticated, academic, emotional, or inspirational.

---

# 4. Preserve the Author's Actual Point of View

Always distinguish between:

## A. What the author actually believes

This may be edited for clarity but should not be changed.

## B. Editorial improvements

These include:

- organization
- transitions
- grammar
- sentence clarity
- removing repetition
- selecting the strongest examples
- improving pacing

These may be changed freely as long as the meaning remains intact.

## C. Claims that require confirmation

Do not turn assumptions, impressions, or incomplete memories into facts.

When necessary, either:

- use more careful language, or
- ask the author to confirm the claim.

For example:

Raw material:

> Students were addicted to their tablets.

If the evidence is behavioral observation rather than a diagnosis, prefer describing what happened:

> Some students kept touching the screen even after I asked them to stop.

Concrete observation is usually stronger than an unsupported label.

---

# 5. Do Not Inflate Small Experiences

A small classroom moment is allowed to remain small.

Do not automatically transform:

> A student asked about Taiwan.

into:

> The interaction demonstrated the transformative power of cross-cultural education.

Do not automatically transform:

> Students participated more than I expected.

into:

> The activity empowered students to take ownership of their learning journey.

Do not automatically transform:

> A student remembered something about Taiwan.

into:

> I successfully built a bridge between two cultures.

The author's definition of success may be modest.

Examples of meaningful outcomes include:

- a normally disengaged student participating
- a student opening a textbook
- students asking questions
- students laughing or singing along
- a student remembering one word
- a student becoming slightly more curious about Taiwan
- an activity working better than expected

Do not enlarge these outcomes to make the article sound more important.

---

# 6. Classroom Reality Over Idealized Teaching

Real classrooms are inconsistent.

Preserve this.

Activities may:

- work in one class and fail in another
- engage high-performing students but not struggling students
- increase participation without improving test results
- be enjoyable without producing clear learning outcomes
- require changes during the lesson
- fail completely

Teachers may also:

- become frustrated
- feel annoyed
- get tired
- enjoy an easier class
- question whether an activity is worth the effort
- change expectations
- improvise
- realize afterward that an idea was imperfect

Do not remove these details to make the author appear more professional.

They are part of the author's teaching voice.

---

# 7. Show Classroom Evidence

Prefer concrete classroom behavior over abstract educational claims.

Instead of:

> The activity increased student engagement.

Prefer evidence such as:

> A student who usually did not bring his textbook borrowed one because he wanted a higher Blooket score.

Instead of:

> Students demonstrated low motivation.

Prefer:

> One of the first questions they asked about the worksheet was, "Is this graded?"

Instead of:

> Students struggled with digital self-regulation.

Prefer:

> Even after I asked everyone to put their hands on their laps, a few students kept clicking the screen.

Let the reader see what happened.

---

# 8. Reflection Should Be Brief

The author is reflective, but reflection does not require long explanation.

A useful default is:

> **Show it. Reflect once if needed. Move on.**

Avoid:

> example → explanation → interpretation → summary → takeaway

Prefer:

> example → brief reflection → next idea

Sometimes the example is strong enough that no reflection is necessary.

Trust the reader.

---

# 9. Do Not Explain the Same Point Twice

This is one of the highest-priority editing rules.

If the anecdote already communicates the idea, do not explain the anecdote again.

If a reflection sentence already communicates the point, do not add another sentence summarizing it.

Ask:

> Does this sentence add a new thought, or does it explain something the reader already understands?

If it only repeats the meaning, remove it.

Avoid restating the same reflection in slightly different words.

During the final publication pass, read the full article specifically for repeated ideas across sections. A sentence may work well on its own but still be unnecessary if the same reflection has already been established elsewhere in the article.

When repetition is found:

- Prefer deleting the weaker or less necessary repetition.
- Do not rewrite both passages simply to make them sound different.
- Do not repeat a reflection because the reader may already understand it.
- Preserve repetition only when the later passage genuinely develops, complicates, or changes the earlier idea.

A paragraph does not always need a concluding sentence.

A section does not always need a takeaway.

---

# 10. Sentence Style and Rhythm

Default to clear, relatively concise sentences.

The author prefers writing that is easy to continue reading.

Do not make sentences longer simply to make the writing sound sophisticated.

Short sentences are welcome when they fit the thought.

For example:

> It was annoying.

> It depends.

> That mattered to me.

> Some classes responded. Others didn't.

A short sentence should usually clarify, emphasize, or replace unnecessary explanation.

Do not create artificial drama through repeated isolated sentences.

Avoid turning the prose into:

> Something happened.  
> It surprised me.  
> That changed everything.  
> And that mattered.

Use sentence length naturally.

---

# 11. Directness, Humor, and Self-Awareness

Preserve moments where the author's natural personality appears.

This may include:

- direct observations
- mild frustration
- dry humor
- self-awareness
- small complaints
- honest admissions

Examples of the author's natural thinking include ideas such as:

> If everyone is going to sit in front of a screen and work by themselves, why come to school to do that?

or:

> I was basically talking to the air.

Do not automatically replace these with professional educational language.

Do not invent jokes or sarcasm when the raw material does not contain them.

The humor should come from the author's observation, not from an AI trying to sound funny.

---

# 12. Avoid AI-Style Rhetorical Habits

The following are not prohibited, but they should not become default writing tools.

## Em dashes

Use em dashes rarely.

Prefer commas, periods, or separate sentences when they work naturally.

Do not use em dashes repeatedly for dramatic pauses.

## Intensifying adverbs

Do not add words such as:

- deeply
- incredibly
- profoundly
- remarkably
- highly
- truly
- powerfully

simply to make an experience sound stronger.

If the detail already shows intensity, let it stand.

## Symmetrical contrast

Do not repeatedly use structures such as:

> not X, but Y

> it isn't X; it's Y

> what mattered wasn't X, but Y

These structures are sometimes useful but become artificial when repeated.

Prefer a direct statement when possible.

## Dramatic one-line reflections

Use them when they genuinely fit the author's thought.

Do not add them simply to create emotional rhythm.

## Automatic paragraph summaries

Avoid endings such as:

> This experience showed me that...

> This highlighted the importance of...

> This reminded me that...

> This demonstrates how...

unless the sentence introduces a genuinely new reflection.

---

# 13. Avoid Marketing Language

The website should not read like promotional content for a school, organization, program, or the author.

Be cautious with language such as:

- transformative
- impactful
- inspiring
- empowering
- innovative
- dynamic
- meaningful
- remarkable
- enriching
- culturally impactful
- life-changing
- powerful journey

These words are not banned.

Use them only when the raw experience genuinely supports them and the author would naturally describe the experience that way.

Never invent positive impact for promotional purposes.

---

# 14. Avoid Unnecessary Academic Language

Educational terminology may be used when it is useful and accurate.

Do not use theory terminology merely to make the author sound knowledgeable.

Avoid replacing a simple observation with academic language.

For example:

Prefer:

> Students were more willing to participate when they could choose what to create.

over:

> Student autonomy facilitated greater intrinsic motivation and learner ownership.

unless the article is specifically discussing those theories.

The author's classroom experience should remain understandable to teachers and general readers.

---

# 15. Theory and Practice

The author's website may increasingly connect educational theory with classroom experience.

Do not retroactively claim that a past lesson was designed according to a theory unless that was actually true.

If the author later learns a theory that helps explain an earlier experience, write honestly about the timeline.

For example:

> When I used this activity, I wasn't thinking about motivation theory. I was simply trying to get more students to participate.

Then the article may explore how later reading changed the author's interpretation.

Theory should help examine experience.

It should not rewrite history.

---

# 16. Teaching Philosophy

Do not force every article to state a teaching philosophy.

When relevant, recurring ideas in the author's teaching include:

- helping students gain confidence
- encouraging students to speak even when they may make mistakes
- making language useful rather than only testable
- using creativity and different forms of expression
- adjusting instruction based on student reactions
- recognizing that different classes respond differently
- using activities such as movement, singing, storytelling, games, and creative tasks when appropriate
- helping students encounter cultures beyond their own

These are tendencies, not slogans.

Do not repeat them mechanically in every article.

---

# 17. Technology

The author's position on classroom technology is intentionally mixed.

Do not simplify it into either:

> Technology is good for education.

or:

> Students use too much technology.

The author recognizes that technology may:

- increase participation
- motivate some struggling students
- provide useful practice
- make classroom management easier
- support efficient learning

while also:

- distract students
- compete for attention
- encourage off-task behavior
- create dependence on digital stimulation
- produce engagement without clear learning

Preserve this tension when relevant.

Do not force a final answer if the author does not have one.

---

# 18. Cross-Cultural Writing

The author often writes from experience across different educational and cultural settings.

Avoid turning cultural differences into broad claims about entire countries.

Prefer:

> At the school I observed...

over:

> American schools...

when the evidence comes from one school.

Prefer:

> In my experience teaching in Taiwan...

over:

> Taiwanese education always...

Distinguish between:

- personal observation
- school-specific practice
- cultural interpretation
- verified broader evidence

Cross-cultural writing should be curious rather than judgmental.

Differences do not automatically need a winner.

---

# 19. Openings

Prefer openings that begin with:

- a real classroom moment
- a student question
- a concrete observation
- a small contradiction
- a question the author genuinely had

For example:

> "Are we using tablets today?"

or:

> The first thing many students did when they walked into the classroom was grab a laptop.

Avoid generic openings such as:

> Education plays an important role in shaping the future of society.

> In today's rapidly changing educational landscape...

> Throughout my professional journey...

Do not begin with a broad philosophical claim unless the raw material genuinely begins there.

---

# 20. Body Structure

There is no mandatory article template.

A useful default pattern is:

> concrete moment or question  
> ↓  
> what happened  
> ↓  
> what the author noticed  
> ↓  
> another experience that complicates the first impression  
> ↓  
> brief reflection  
> ↓  
> stop when the thought is complete

Sections should exist because the content changes direction, not because a blog article is expected to have a certain number of headings.

Do not force every article into "three key takeaways."

---

# 21. Endings

The author generally prefers endings that are short and clear.

Do not automatically summarize the whole article.

Do not repeat every major point.

Do not force:

- a lesson for all teachers
- an inspirational message
- a call to action
- an audience question
- a universal educational conclusion

An article may end with:

- the author's current position
- an unresolved question
- a small observation
- a modest conclusion
- a concrete classroom image
- a lightly humorous thought

Once the thought is complete, stop.

A strong ending does not need to sound important.

---

# 22. Questions

Rhetorical questions are appropriate when they reflect questions the author genuinely asks herself.

Do not add many rhetorical questions simply to make the article conversational.

A question should usually:

- introduce a real uncertainty
- move the thinking forward
- reveal the author's internal reaction

Avoid stacking several rhetorical questions when one is enough.

---

# 23. English Word Choice

Prefer common, natural English when it communicates the idea accurately.

Do not automatically replace simple words with sophisticated synonyms.

Prefer:

- use over utilize
- help over facilitate
- show over demonstrate when appropriate
- try over implement when appropriate
- change over transform when that is the actual meaning

Educational terminology is acceptable when it is the clearest word.

Vocabulary should sound like an educated teacher speaking naturally, not like a journal article or corporate report.

---

# 24. Chinese-to-English Editing

When the raw material contains Chinese, preserve the idea rather than the literal sentence.

Chinese expressions may be:

- more direct
- more compressed
- humorous in a way that does not translate literally
- emotionally different when translated word for word

Find the natural English equivalent that preserves the function of the original sentence.

For example, a direct Chinese thought such as:

> 如果都在用平板，那來學校幹嘛？

should remain direct in English.

It should not automatically become a philosophical sentence such as:

> What unique pedagogical value does the physical classroom provide in a technology-mediated learning environment?

Preserve the thought, not the surface grammar.

---

# 25. Facts, Memory, and Uncertainty

Never invent:

- student reactions
- dialogue
- lesson outcomes
- student ages
- classroom details
- test results
- teaching methods
- motivations
- cultural explanations

If the author cannot remember a detail, do not fill it in because it would make the story better.

If the author says:

> I think...

> probably...

> based on what I remember...

preserve an appropriate level of uncertainty.

If later evidence contradicts an earlier interpretation, update the article.

Factual accuracy is more important than narrative neatness.

---

# 26. Do Not Manufacture a Success Story

Do not assume every article needs:

> problem → struggle → breakthrough → success → lesson

Sometimes:

- the lesson did not work
- some students remained disengaged
- the author still does not know the answer
- an activity improved participation but not learning
- nothing dramatic changed

That is acceptable.

The author's writing becomes more credible when outcomes remain proportional to what actually happened.

---

# 27. Article Scope

One experience may produce multiple articles.

Do not put every related classroom story into one article simply because it happened during the same period.

Each article should have a clear central question or idea.

For example, the author's Alabama experience may support separate articles about:

- teaching students who did not choose Mandarin
- teaching Taiwanese night-market culture
- teaching Chinese characters
- making ordinary textbook topics more personal
- comparing school routines
- adapting activities across different classes

Use only the experiences that serve the current article.

Save other useful stories for future articles.

This prevents repetition and keeps each article focused.

---

# 28. Editing Workflow

When editing substantial raw material, internally separate:

### Original viewpoint

What is the author actually saying?

### Editorial adjustment

What needs to change for clarity, structure, English, or pacing?

### Confirmation needed

Is there any factual claim, interpretation, quotation, or tone decision that should not be assumed?

Do not silently resolve uncertainty simply because one version creates a stronger article.

When discussing major edits with the author, make these distinctions visible when useful.

---

# 29. What AI May Change Freely

AI may generally:

- correct grammar
- improve natural English
- reorder paragraphs
- remove repetition
- shorten explanations
- choose stronger examples from supplied material
- improve transitions
- create useful headings
- combine repetitive sentences
- split difficult sentences
- translate Chinese ideas naturally into English

provided that these changes do not alter meaning.

---

# 30. What AI Must Not Change Without Good Reason

AI must not silently:

- make an experience more successful
- make the author more emotional
- make the author more certain
- create student reactions
- invent dialogue
- exaggerate educational impact
- add a teaching philosophy that was not present
- convert observation into research claims
- turn one school into a claim about a country
- claim that an activity was based on a theory when it was not
- create a moral lesson
- make the author sound more inspirational
- make the author sound more academically sophisticated
- remove contradictions simply to create a cleaner argument

Polish language aggressively if useful.

Polish reality conservatively.

---

# 31. Before / After Calibration Examples

## Example 1: Avoid inflated meaning

Avoid:

> This interaction demonstrated the transformative power of cross-cultural learning.

Prefer:

> They asked questions about Taiwan. That was enough for me.

---

## Example 2: Trust the example

Avoid:

> The student borrowed a textbook to search for vocabulary. This demonstrated that gamification successfully increased his engagement and encouraged active participation in the learning process.

Prefer:

> He borrowed a textbook, looked for the words, and participated.

If a reflection is needed:

> That mattered to me.

---

## Example 3: Do not over-explain

Avoid:

> Some students continued clicking their screens while I was speaking. This made it difficult to regain their attention and showed me that technology can become a distraction when students lack self-regulation.

Prefer:

> Some students kept clicking even while I was talking. I could stop the lesson every time it happened, but then we would never finish anything.

---

## Example 4: Preserve directness

Avoid:

> I began questioning the pedagogical value of individual screen-based activities in a physical classroom environment.

Prefer:

> If everyone is going to sit in front of a screen and work by themselves, why come to school to do that?

---

## Example 5: Do not manufacture inspiration

Avoid:

> Despite the challenges, the experience strengthened my commitment to becoming a cultural bridge and inspiring future generations through language education.

Prefer:

> If a few students left remembering something about Taiwan or Mandarin, that was enough for me.

---

## Example 6: Let unsuccessful outcomes remain unsuccessful

Avoid:

> Although test performance remained challenging, the activity planted an important seed for future learning.

Prefer:

> Based on his test results, he probably did not remember those words.

Then continue only if there is something genuinely different worth saying.

---

# 32. Reference Pieces

The following articles currently represent the closest examples of the intended voice:

1. **My Students Love Using Tablets. I'm Not Sure I Do. — Version 3**
2. **Teaching Chinese to Students Who Didn't Choose to Learn Chinese — refined version**

Use these as calibration references, not templates.

Do not copy:

- their exact structure
- their sentence patterns
- their headings
- their endings
- their number of sections

Instead, observe their general qualities:

- readable pacing
- concrete classroom evidence
- relatively short sentences
- limited explanation
- direct reflection
- honest uncertainty
- small outcomes kept small
- personality without forced humor
- no inspirational conclusion

---

# 33. Updating This Guide

This guide should evolve through actual writing.

Do not create a permanent rule because of one isolated sentence correction.

Add or change a rule when:

1. the author explicitly identifies it as a general writing preference, or
2. the same preference appears repeatedly across different articles.

Future article types may reveal different needs, especially:

- theory-and-practice reflections
- graduate-school reading reflections
- research-informed education articles
- longer personal narratives

Allow the guide to evolve when real examples justify the change.

Do not predict every future writing situation now.

---

# Final Editing Test

Before an article is considered publication-ready, perform one full-article repetition pass checking for:

- the same reflection stated more than once
- conclusions that repeat an earlier conclusion
- over-explanation of an idea the article has already established
- similar sentences that survived from different rounds of editing

Before delivering an edited article, ask:

### Meaning
Did I preserve what the author actually meant?

### Evidence
Did I accidentally turn an observation into a stronger claim?

### Voice
Does this sound like the author with stronger English, or like an education content writer?

### Proportion
Did I make a small classroom moment sound more important than it was?

### Readability
Would the author want to keep reading?

### Repetition
Did I explain anything the reader already understood?

### AI rhythm
Did I rely too heavily on em dashes, adverbs, symmetrical contrasts, dramatic one-liners, or automatic summary sentences?

### Personality
Did I accidentally polish away directness, humor, frustration, or uncertainty that belonged to the author?

### Ending
Did I stop when the thought was complete?

If the answer to any of these raises concern, revise before delivering.

The final goal is simple:

> **Clearer English. Better structure. Same person.**
