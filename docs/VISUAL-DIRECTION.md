# Visual Direction

## Visual Identity

The website should feel calm, thoughtful, editorial, personal, educational, and human-centered. It should present professional work with warmth and clarity while allowing reflection, teaching, and lived experience to remain central.

Avoid corporate branding, startup landing-page patterns, generic blog templates, and excessive decoration. The site should not feel promotional or visually performative.

## Layout Principles

- Use generous whitespace to create a comfortable pace and give content room to breathe.
- Establish a clear hierarchy so visitors can understand each page quickly.
- Let typography carry the design before introducing additional visual elements.
- Use visual accents with restraint and only when they strengthen the composition or meaning.
- Keep the content as the primary focus of every page.

## Typography

- Use serif display type for headings to support the established editorial character.
- Keep body text highly readable, with a comfortable measure, spacing, and line height for long-form reading.
- Create distinction through hierarchy, scale, weight, and spacing rather than decoration.
- Avoid excessive type effects, ornamental treatments, or competing font styles.

## Organic Shape Language

The established visual identity uses three muted geometric circular forms in soft yellow, muted green, and soft purple. They are a recurring compositional language, not a logo, icon set, or a fixed template to copy onto every page. Their placement can vary by page while preserving the relationship between generous whitespace, large typography, and restrained color.

The shapes can suggest connection, learning, memory, and creativity. Their role is compositional: they can establish page identity, balance whitespace, or create a meaningful relationship between content areas.

- Shapes should have a clear compositional purpose.
- Their placement, scale, and relationship to content should feel intentional.
- They should not become random decoration or compete with the page's meaning.
- Do not turn the shapes into a separate logo or icon system.
- Keep forms behind or beside readable content; they must never reduce text contrast, obscure controls, or make a composition feel decorative by default.

## Color Usage

The established accent palette uses:

- Soft yellow
- Muted green
- Soft purple

Use these colors as subtle accents within the existing neutral visual system. Preserve the calm editorial feeling, maintain appropriate contrast for content and controls, and avoid saturated or attention-seeking color treatments.

## Image Usage

Images should support meaning and strengthen a visitor's understanding of the person, experience, or material being presented.

Appropriate uses include:

- A circular personal portrait where it supports the page's identity and context
- Classroom experiences presented as rectangular editorial photography, with appropriate permission and privacy protection
- Teaching materials
- Educational moments
- Cultural experiences
- Meaningful visuals connected to an article

Avoid stock photography, decorative thumbnails, and images added only to fill space. Images are optional and are not required for every page or article. When used, they must follow the site's accessibility, licensing, and privacy requirements.

Preserve the authentic, natural character of approved photography. Do not turn classroom photographs into decorative circles, use heavy filters, or replace them with generic imagery merely to create visual consistency.

## Page-Specific Guidance

### Homepage

The homepage establishes identity and introduces the website's focus. It can use a stronger visual presence than interior pages while keeping the introduction and navigation paths clear.

### About

The About page presents personal background and an educational journey. Personal images are appropriate when they add useful context and remain consistent with the page's concise, professional tone.

### Writing Archive

The Writing archive is an editorial content index. The broader visual identity may appear subtly to distinguish the page, but article titles, descriptions, dates, and navigation remain primary.

### Article Pages

Article pages prioritize the reading experience. Use a comfortable reading width, clear heading hierarchy, and minimal distraction. Avoid visual elements that interrupt or compete with the writing.

## Components to Avoid

The site intentionally avoids:

- Generic blog cards
- Large promotional banners
- Excessive calls to action
- Tag-heavy interfaces
- Unnecessary icons
- Crowded layouts

## Motion and change discipline

Motion, where it already exists, should remain restrained and support the reading experience; it must respect reduced-motion preferences. Make visual changes in small, reversible steps, and do not casually redesign approved visual compositions or replace bespoke layouts with generic components.

## Future Extensions

Future pages, components, content types, and visual features should preserve the site's editorial feeling, personal reflection, and calm visual language. New elements should extend the established principles rather than introduce a competing design system. Their purpose should be clear, and the simplest coherent expression should be preferred.
